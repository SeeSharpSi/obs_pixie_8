# [My Defense of Divine Simplicity](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wlpdpAnVwgI&t=440s) 
* Gavin Ortlund 

## What is Divine Simplicity? (7:58) 
* Dictionary definition: 
	* Does not mean that God is not complicated, it means that God is not composite 
	* That is to say God is not made up of parts (spatial, temporal, metaphysical, ontological) 
	* You can't break God down into some prior ontological elements 
	* God is absolutely simple 
* Historically: 
	* This is pressed out differently between the East and the West and how you define it 
	* In terms of how ambitious they are and how they're put to use for a theological purpose 
	* In the West you get sort of a stronger account, sometimes called the Identity Thesis or the Identity Account 
		* Which basically, in addition to making the negative claim that there's no parts in God, there's the positive claim that God is identical to his existence and to his attributes 
		* As to the second part, God is not merely righteous, He is righteousness; God is not merely loving, He is love. He is identical to each of His attributes 
		* Another way to put it: all that is in God is God. There is nothing in God that is not identical to Himself 
		* Or: God is what He has 
		* Seen by [[Saint Augustine|Augustine]], Anselm, Aquinas. . . (the West) 
		* "Divine wisdom is the same as divine essence in every way in which the divine essence is the same as itself. Similarly, for divine goodness and justice. Nor is there any distinction at all, or even any nonidentity there in the nature of the thing" (William of Ockham) 
	* The Eastern tradition is less ambitious than that 
		* You definitely have the strong denial of any parts in God 
		* But you don't always have the affirmation that God is identical to His attributes 
		* And it seems like, generally speaking, there is a denial or pushing in the other direction of that 
		* Book recommendation: [link](https://a.co/d/9CTnjzv) 

## Why has this doctrine been seen to be so important (14:28) 
* It's seen to protect other things that are integral to the nature of God 
	* Namely: Divine [Aseity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aseity) and Divine Absoluteness 
	* Divine Aseity: God exists from Himself and that everything He has is from Himself. He does not derive in any way from anything external from Himself 
	* Divine Absoluteness: God is not conditioned by anything external to Himself 
* When you get into the historical account of this doctrine you get into different concerns 
* Modernly, it's seen as a philosophical consequence 
* Historical, in the witness and the worship of the Church Divine Simplicity was absolutely essential for the Church simply being the Church 
	* It was part of the worship of the Church 
	* It was part of the apologetics of the Church 
	* Example: Athenagoras, when he's writing a letter to the Roman emperors, he's defending Christian theism over polytheism with Divine Simplicity 
		* For him, to accept a simple, non-composite God was part and parcel with him rejecting the polytheism of his day 
	* Boethius, writing his famous "Constellation of Philosophy," uses Divine Simplicity to establish that the divine nature is beautiful and stable 
	* A simple God leads you to a more stable and beautiful conception of God 
	* Another text: Bonaventure, his work into mystical theology "The Journey into the Mind of God," uses Divine Simplicity in huge ways, and really grounds the whole creator-creation relationship that is the premise of his mystical approach to God 
		* "Because [the divine nature] is the most simple and the greatest, it is wholly within all things and wholly outside them; hence it is 'the intelligible sphere, whose center is everywhere and whose circumference is nowhere'" 
* The contemporary view of Divine Simplicity is that it's a static doctrine that cuts us off, slices us off, from meaningful dynamic relationship to God 
	* For the pre-modern tradition it was the exact opposite 
* Because God is simple, he has all communicability within Himself (Bonaventure) 
	* In other words, you get this sense that, because God is simple, He can be fully present to every creature in every way 
	* He's outside all things, but also inside all things 
* For the Church, Divine Simplicity has been how you construe the creator-creation relationship 
	* It's one of the vital underpinnings of that 

## How do we make sense of this? (21:30) 
* Plantinga says things like "since God is identical to His attributes, are His attributes identical to each other?" 
	* "How can you have a personal God who's identical to abstract qualities like wisdom?" 
	* Christians thought of this historically 
* They understood God to exist in a different ontological framework 
	* God is a fundamentally different kind of being than everything else 
	* One of the huge influences on the Church's understanding of Divine Simplicity was Plotinus; he made an important innovation in talking about "the one" as "being before being" (pre-ontological) 
	* Pseudo-Dionysius - someone people thought was the real Acts 17 Dionysius, so people took him seriously (the reformers don't like him) - says that God is supra essential; God is in a different ontological framework. He's in a category of His own 
	* Pseudo-Dionysius calls God "mind beyond mind, word beyond speech." He says he transcends itself, He transcends every category of existence 
		* "The one cause of all things is not one of the many things in the world but actually precedes oneness and multiplicity and indeed defines oneness and multiplicity" (Pseudo-Dionysius, in the context of explaining how God can be one-in-three) 
	* "...the super-essential essence, the Godhead that is more than God, the beginning that is above beginning..." (John of Damascus) 
	* "God is not related to creatures as though belonging to a different genus, but as transcending every genus, and as the principle of all genera" (Thomas Aquinas) 
	* God doesn't fit into our categories, He's entirely a league of His own 
* In much of the contemporary philosophical discussion, God is approached as though He exists within a larger structure we call reality. In classical theology, there's the tendency to approach reality as itself subsisting within the being of God 
	* Shorter: God is not within reality, reality is within God 
	* God is not just a thing among other things. God simply is 
* Divine Simplicity highlights to Godness of God 
	* His absolute uniqueness and transcendence 
	* One consequence of that is a sort of linguistic cautioning 
	* Like, why does the modern approach (Plantinga) seem so different than the historical (Aquinas) 
		* There's a cautioning influence from these premodern resources saying "why would we assume that the divine nature plays by the same rules as the way other creatures relate to their own attributes?" 
		* Here's how Augustine puts the caution: "Whatever is said of a nature, unchangeable, invisible and having life absolutely and sufficient to itself, must not be measured after the custome of things visible, and changeable, and mortal, or not self-sufficient" 
		* Here's how Anselm puts it: "You ... do not exist in a place or a time; rather, all things exist in you. For nothing contains you, but you contain all things" 
		* The modern claims seem to believe that God will relate to His attributes in the way that creatures will relate to their attributes; that God is on the same ontological plain as everything else 

## How do we reconcile divine simplicity with the fact that there's three persons in the Godhead? (28:03) 
* A lot of what I'm doing in this video is trying to contrast a lot of the contemporary tendencies in philosophical approach to Divine Simplicity with those to classical Christian tradition 
* The tendency is to think that the Trinity is as odds with Divine Simplicity 
	* It's certainly the way Jewish and Muslim philosophers thought 
* In his book "The Guide for the Perplexed," Moses-Maimonides has a well-known treatment for Divine Simplicity 
	* He pushes it as far as to say "God has no essential attributes" 
	* "The closest you can get to cognizing God is picturing whiteness" 
	* Why whiteness if God can't be attached to anything? 
* Classical theologians didn't spend that much time trying to reconcile Divine Simplicity and the Trinity 
* There's an enormous amount of caution in the classical tradition about how we speak about the divine essence 
	* The greater interest of classical theologians was to use Divine Simplicity to ground the Trinity as purely monotheistic 
	* Today people often use perichoresis - the idea of mutual indwelling, or interpenetration, among the persons of the Godhead - as a way to retain monotheism while you have the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit; three persons 
		* That's kinda on the backfoot in the tradition, it seems 
		* I don't think you really get perichoresis applied to the divine persons until John of Damascus in the 7th century 
		* It originally had to do with the two natures of Christ 
		* Tradition's main way of dealing with this problem was Divine Simplicity 
	* "How does one and one not equal two Gods? Because we speak of the emperor, and the emperor's image - but not two emperors. ==Since the divine nature is not composed of parts, union of the persons is accomplished by partaking of the whole==" (Basil) 
		* That last sentence is a great example of how Divine Simplicity was used to ground the Trinity as monotheistic in traditional theology 
		* Aquinas basically says "you can have a plurality of relations in God, but not a plurality of absolute things. A plurality of relations does not import compositions onto the divine essence" 
	* It's striking how little time is spent in how you reconcile Divine Simplicity and the Trinity. The extent this question comes up is primarily a caution 
		* "When the Lord taught us the doctrine of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, he did not make arithmetic a part of this gift... The Unapproachable One is beyond numbers, wisest sirs; imitate the reverence shown by the Hebrews of old to the unutterable name of God" (Basil) 
		* This is sort of a caution, or even rebuke, to gesturing to divine transcendence, divine uniqueness, He's beyond numbers, God is in His own ontological framework, so there's not even much of a need to reconcile the Trinity to Divine Simplicity 
* Some have said Divine Simplicity makes God a little scary 
* Is this irrational? Logical doesn't apply to God? 
	* We're not saying that God defies logic, we're saying that God defines logic as its source and therefor has a unique relation to it 
	* God isn't just an arbitrary exception to the rules, it's that He's a different kind of thing all together 
* Divine Simplicity leads to divine agnosticism 
	* "We make the divine essence so different that we really don't know what God is like" 
	* Again, it's a little bit of nuance here. Not that God's goodness has no relation to our goodness. It just has a unique relation to our conception of goodness because He's the source of goodness 
	* We can call God "good," but can recognize that He's not good in the exact same way as other things are good 
	* Basically, we're saying that our language doesn't map on to God in a univocal way - sort of a one-to-one correspondence - but, that doesn't mean, on the other extreme, it has an equivocal relationship with God. What we're talking about is an analogical relationship with God 
	* What that ultimately leaves us with is not an obliterated knowledge of God, but chastened, humbled knowledge of God 

## Video close (35:59) 
* That is what I ultimately take from Divine Simplicity 
* It's a reminder of the Godness of God 
* It humbles us, to the dust, under the absolute transcendence and lordship of God; He is like nothing else 
* Here's a quote that captures something of the import and caution that Divine Simplicity impresses upon us 
	* "I know they dazzle our eyes and flow in and out of one another like eddies on a river, and nothing that is said clearly can be said truly about them. Holy places are dark places. It is life and strength, not knowledge and words, that we get in them. Holy wisdom is not clear and thin like water, but thick and dark like blood" (C.S. Lewis, _Til We Have Faces_) 
	* Obviously the content of this is troubling, but the willingness to embrace counterintuitive ways of thinking when we're dealing with the divine essence because it's something utterly unique 
* "Our starting point must be that God cannot be named" (Gregory of Nazianzus) 
* That can seem bracing, but there is a positive way of thinking about that. That is both Biblical, rooted in the classical instincts of the Church, and very productive theologically in this sort of chastened cautioned position it leaves us as creatures relating to the creator because He is like nothing else that we know and therefore we are entirely dependent upon His revelation to understand Him and to speak of Him
