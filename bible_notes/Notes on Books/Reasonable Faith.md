## **Historical Background** 

### _Medieval_ (pg. 29) 
* In our historical survey, let's first look at Augustine (354-430) and Thomas Aquinas (1224-1274). Their approaches were determinative for the Middle Ages. 

### **Augustine** (354-430) 
* His views evolved over the years, so they can be hard to interpret 
* 29.3 sometimes seems like a strict authoritarian (held that scripture has the prime, most divine source of authority) 
	* Scripture is completely void of error 
	* Scripture is to be believed absolutely 
* Such a belief seems to imply that reason has no role in the justification of belief 
	* Read the septuagint's version of Is. 7:9 - "to believe, you must first understand"
	* He is seen as often teaching that faith must be held to understand, and/or that faith is required before knowledge 
* Still, he often showed that he was not an unqualified authoritarian
	* He said that faith and reason cooperate in bringing someone do belief  
	* While belief in scripture would lend towards it being the ultimate authority, people have to arrive at that view first before exploring its authoritative assertions 
* Big question: why accept the authority of the past? 
	* Augustine did not have the historical knowledge present to us today. So instead of relying on that for his belief, he relied on the "miracle" of God's earthly church 
	* This doesn't mean he holds the Church's authority over scripture. Rather, the church's existence makes evident the truth of scripture, which in turn causes him to believe in its authority 
	* This is actually not authoritarian at all; rather, it's based on a present miracle 
	* But Augustine promoted the authority of scripture to enough of an extent to set the tone for subsequent medieval theology 
### **Thomas Aquinas** (1224-1274) 
* 32 His _Summa contra gentiles_, written to combat Greco-Arabic philosophy, is the greatest apologetic work of the Middle Ages and so merits our attention 
	* Showed there are truths that surpass our human understanding (i.e. the trinity) 
	* Still, many truths lie within the grasp of human reason (i.e. the existence of God) 
* In volumes 1-3, he tackles human-graspable topics like the existence of God 
* In the fourth volume, he starts to tackle ungraspable things like the trinity, sacraments, the incarnation, etc. 
* This makes Aquinas seem authoritative, and outside of reason 
	* he's not, and carefully treads the issue 
	* he makes clear that these things are not empirically evident. Though he makes no suggestion that truths of faith surpass Aristotelian logic 
	* Example: God can be proved from His effects, while the trinity relies solely on logic and reason 
* Believed that we should only use arguments that prove with certainty, rather than probability. A case for probability will only reaffirm non-believers in their unbelief 
* Aquinas's procedure can be summarized in three steps: 
	1. Fulfilled prophecies and miracles make it credible that the Scriptures taken together as a whole are a revelation from God
	2. As a revelation from God, Scripture is absolutely authoritative
	3. Therefore, those doctrines taught by Scripture that are neither demonstrably provable nor empirically evident may be accepted by faith on the authority of Scripture 
* Thus, Aquinas can say that an opponent may be convinced of the truths of faith on the basis of the authority of Scripture as confirmed by God with miracles/prophecy 
* This raises the question: how do we know that the purported miracles or fulfilled prophecy ever took place? 
	* Medieval thinkers, lacking the historical method, could not answer this question 
	* About the only argument was Augustine's indirect proof from the miracle of the church 

### _The Enlightenment_ (pg. 34) 
* Known as the Age of Reason. Still, the relationship between faith and reason was not completely agreed upon 

### **John Locke** (1632-1704) 
* 34.2 Believed that religious belief required a foundation, and attempted to provide one 
* Gave a cosmological argument for the existence of God, and believed that existence of God is "the most obvious truth that reason discovers," having evidence "equal to mathematical certainty" 
* When one moves beyond matters of demonstrable reason into matters of faith, Locke insisted that ==revealed truths cannot contradict reason== as well as truths unattainable by reason 
* Reason gives greater certainty of these than revelation 
* Truths of faith cannot contradict truths of reason, because truths of reason will always be more certain than purported revelations of faith that contradict reason 
* No proposition contrary to reason can be accepted as divine revelation 
* While we know a revelation from God is absolutely true, it lies within the scope of reason to determine if a supposed revelation really is from God to determine its meaning 
* Revelation is natural reason enlarged by a new set of discoveries communicated by God immediately, which reason vouches the truth of by the testimony and proofs it gives that they come from God. So that he that takes away reason to make way for revelation, puts out the light of both; and does much the same as if he would persuade a man to put out his eyes, the better to receive the remote light of an invisible star by a telescope (Ibid., 4.19.4).

### **Alvin Plantinga** (1932-present) 
* 39.3 Appealing to what he (erroneously, I think) calls the Reformed objection to natural theology, Alvin Plantinga has launched a sustained attack on theological rationalism. Plantinga maintains that belief in God and in the central doctrines of Christianity is both rational and warranted wholly apart from any evidential foundations for belief 
	* This brings him into conflict what what he calls the evidentialist objection to theistic belief 
	* According the the evidentialist, one is rationally justified in believing a proposition to be true only if that proposition is either foundational to knowledge or is established by evidence that is ultimately based on such a foundation 
	* According to this viewpoint, since the proposition "God exists" is not foundational, it would be irrational to believe this proposition apart from rational evidence for its truth 
* But Plantinga asks why can't the proposition "God exists" be itself part of the foundation, so that no rational evidence is necessary? 
	* The evidentialist replies that only propositions that are properly basic can be part of the foundation of knowledge 
	* What, then, are the criteria that determine whether or not a proposition is properly basic? 
	* Typically the evidentialist asserts that only propositions that are self-evident or incorrigible are properly basic (e.g. the proposition "The sum of the squares of two sides of a right triangle is equal to the square of the hypotenuse" is self-evidently true. Similarly, the proposition "I feel pain" is incorrigibly true, since even if I am only imagining my injury, it is still true that I _feel_ pain) 
	* Since the proposition "God exists" is neither self-evident nor incorrigible, it is not properly basic and therefore requires evidence if it is to be believed. To believe this proposition without evidence is therefore irrational 
* Plantinga does not deny that self-evident and incorrigible propositions are properly basic, but he does ask how we know that these are the _only_ properly basic propositions or beliefs 
	* If they are, we are all irrational, since we commonly accept numerous beliefs that are not based on evidence and that are neither self-evident nor incorrigible (e.g. the belief that the world was not created 5 minutes ago) 
* Plantinga maintains that belief of God is properly basic 
	* Man has an innate, natural capacity to apprehend God's existence even as he has a natural capacity to accept truths of perception (like "I see a tree") 
	* Give the appropriate circumstances - such as moments of guilt, gratitude, or a sense of God's handiwork in nature - man naturally apprehends God's existence 
	* In the same way that certain perceptual beliefs, like "I see a tree," are properly basic given the appropriate circumstances, so belief in God is properly basic in appropriate circumstances 
	* Neither the tree's existence nor God's existence is _inferred_ from one's experience of the circumstances. But being in the appropriate circumstances is what renders one's belief _properly_ basic; the belief would be irrational were it to be held under inappropriate circumstances 
	* Thus, the basic belief that God exists is not arbitrary, since it is properly held only by a person placed in appropriate circumstances 
* Taking the belief in God as properly basic does not commit one to the relativistic view that virtually any belief can be properly basic for a normal adult 
	* In the absence of appropriate circumstances, various beliefs taken as basic by certain persons will be arbitrarily and irrationally held 
	* Even in the absence of an adequate criterion of proper basicality to replace the flawed evidentialist criterion, the fact is that we can know that some beliefs are just not _properly_ basic 
	* Thus, the Christian who takes belief in God as properly basic can legitimately reject the proper basicality of other beliefs 
* Plantinga thus insists that his epistemology is not fideistic; the deliverances of reason include not only the inferred propositions, but also properly basic propositions 
	* God has so constructed us that we naturally form the belief in his existence under appropriate circumstances, just as we do the belief in perceptual objects, the reality of the past, and so forth 
	* Hence, belief in God is among the deliverances of reason, not faith

### **Deductive Arguments** 
* 52.3 In sound of deductive argument, the conclusion follows inevitably from the premises 
* The two prerequisites of a sound deductive argument are that the premises be true and the logic be valid
* An example of an invalid argument would be: 
	1. If God exists, objective moral values exist 
	2. Objective moral values exist 
	3. Therefore, God exists 
* This commits the fallacy of "[affirming the consequent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affirming_the_consequent)" 

### **Inductive Arguments 
* 52.8 An inductive argument is an argument of which the premises may be true and the logical inferences valid but the conclusion still be false 
* 53.1 Example of a sound inductive argument: 
	1. Groups A, B, C were composed of similar persons suffering from the same disease 
	2. Group A was administered a certain new drug, Group B was administered a placebo, and Group C was not given any treatment 
	3. The rate of death was subsequently lower in Group A by 75% in comparison with both groups B and C 
	4. Therefore, the new drug is effective in reducing the death rate of said disease 
* This is inductively true, though not inevitably true 
	* Maybe the people in Group A were just lucky or some unknown variable caused their improvement 
* 54.5 A different approach to indictive reasoning which is apt to be more useful in apologetics is provided by inference to the best explanation 
	* In this, we are confronted with certain data to be explained 
	* We assemble a pool of explanations of said data 
	* We then select the option from this pool that, if true, best explains the data 
* What it means to be "the best" explanation is disputed, but includes explanatory scope, explanatory power, ad hoc-ness, etc.

If you're sincerely seeking God, then God will give you assurance that the gospel is true.
